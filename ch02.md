## concept Markets and the meaning of demand | 38-39
- * A market brings buyers and sellers together to settle price and quantity; it may be local, regional, national, international or online, and may be a goods, factor or financial market.
- * def Demand :: The quantity buyers are both willing and able to purchase at a given price over a given period - desire without purchasing power (or ability without desire) is not demand.
- eg A poor farmer who wants a harvester but cannot pay, or a student who can afford a burger but does not want one - neither creates demand.
- def Demand schedule vs demand curve :: The schedule is a table of quantities demanded at different prices; the curve plots the same relationship on a graph.
- Convention: price on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal axis; the curve need not be smooth, only generally downward sloping.

## formula Law of demand and market demand | 39-42
- * Ceteris paribus, a rise in price contracts quantity demanded and a fall extends it - an inverse relationship, so the curve slopes down.
- * Two reasons: income effect (a higher price cuts the purchasing power of income) and substitution effect (the good becomes dearer than alternatives, so buyers switch).
- * Held constant: consumers' income, population, prices of substitutes and complements, taste / habit / fashion / weather, price expectations, advertising.
- eg Chicken schedule: Rs 500 -> 10 kg, Rs 400 -> 20 kg, Rs 300 -> 35 kg, Rs 200 -> 55 kg, Rs 100 -> 80 kg.
- x Exceptions: basic necessities (sugar, rice, wheat bought regardless), unique / distinctive goods (higher price raises demand for some groups), rising income, consumer ignorance of cheaper prices elsewhere, fear of future shortage.
- def Giffen paradox :: Sir Robert Giffen (19th century) saw bread demand fall when its price fell - the gain in real income let consumers move to better-quality bread.
- Practical uses: price setting, firm planning, and helping the finance minister judge whether a tax-driven price rise will shrink demand so much that revenue barely grows.
- ! Wages are NOT a condition of the law of demand (they belong to supply).
- * Market demand = the sum of every individual's quantity demanded at each price (horizontal summation of individual curves).
- f Market quantity demanded at price P :: Qm = QA + QB + QC + ... :: Q = each buyer's quantity at the same price :: At Rs 300: 35 + 25 + 20 = 80 kg; at Rs 100: 80 + 60 + 45 = 185 kg :: Add quantities across buyers, never prices
| Price (Rs / kg) | Mr A | Mr B | Mr C | Market |
| 500 | 10 | 5 | 6 | 21 |
| 400 | 20 | 10 | 12 | 42 |
| 300 | 35 | 25 | 20 | 80 |
| 200 | 55 | 40 | 30 | 125 |
| 100 | 80 | 60 | 45 | 185 |

## compare Movement along vs shift of the demand curve | 42-43
| Point | Movement along the curve | Shift of the curve |
| * Cause | Change in the good's OWN price | Change in anything else - income, taste, weather, population, substitute prices, advertising |
| Name | Extension / contraction; change in quantity demanded | Rise / fall; change in demand |
| Picture | Slide from point a to b on one curve | Whole curve moves right (rise) or left (fall) |
| Meaning | More bought only because it is cheaper | More (or less) bought at every price |
- eg Hot weather -> more room coolers bought at every price = rise in demand (shift right).
- eg Winter raises demand for tea and coffee; summer favours soft drinks; a bigger town population raises demand for houses.
- * A rise in a substitute's price raises demand for the other good; a fall in a substitute's price lowers it.
- def Substitutes vs complements :: Substitutes can replace each other; complements are used together.
- def Direct vs derived demand :: Direct demand is for consumer goods bought to satisfy wants; derived demand is for factors of production and capital goods, needed because of the goods they help make.

## compare Supply, law of supply and supply shifts | 44-46
- * def Supply :: The quantity of a good actually offered for sale at a given price and moment - not the whole stock in existence.
- eg A farmer stores 1,000 tons of rice and offers 300 tons at Rs 650 per ton: 300 tons is supply, 700 tons is stock.
- * Law of supply: ceteris paribus, quantity supplied extends as price rises and contracts as price falls - a direct relationship, so the curve slopes up.
- eg Rice supply schedule: Rs 10 -> 0, Rs 20 -> 5, Rs 30 -> 15, Rs 40 -> 30, Rs 50 -> 50, Rs 60 -> 65 tons.
- Held constant: cost of production, technology, taxes and subsidies, input prices, prices of other goods.
- ! Consumer income is NOT a condition of the law of supply.
- Smooth upward supply curves apply to product markets; factor-market supply curves can take other shapes (land supply is vertical - self-test).
| Point | Change in quantity supplied | Change in supply |
| * Cause | The product's own price | Input / resource prices, technology, taxes and subsidies, prices of other goods |
| Picture | Movement along the existing curve | Curve shifts right (rise) or left (fall) |
- * Higher input prices or wages raise cost, squeeze profit and shift supply left; better technology uses fewer inputs and shifts it right.
- * Taxes raise cost and cut supply; subsidies do the reverse.
- eg Same plant can make school bags or ladies' handbags - if school bags become more profitable, handbag supply falls.
- ! Higher labour productivity shifts supply right; a rise in indirect taxes shifts it left; equal-sized rises in indirect tax and subsidy leave it unchanged.

## formula Market equilibrium | 47-48
- * Two prices exist: equilibrium (market) price set by demand and supply, and regulated price set by government.
- * def Equilibrium :: A state where no agent wants to change plans and all plans can be carried out together - planned demand equals planned supply (Qd = Qs).
- f Equilibrium condition :: Qd = Qs :: Qd = quantity demanded, Qs = quantity supplied at a price :: Rice table: at Rs 30 thousand per ton Qd = 10 = Qs -> equilibrium; at 40, Qd 7 < Qs 13 -> surplus of 6; at 20, Qd 14 > Qs 6 -> shortage of 8 :: Surplus = Qs - Qd above equilibrium; shortage = Qd - Qs below it
| Price (Rs 000 / ton) | Qd | Qs | Position | Price pressure |
| 50 | 5 | 15 | Surplus 10 | Down |
| 40 | 7 | 13 | Surplus 6 | Down |
| * 30 | 10 | 10 | Equilibrium | None |
| 20 | 14 | 6 | Shortage 8 | Up |
| 10 | 19 | 1 | Shortage 18 | Up |
- * Price mechanism: a shortage makes buyers compete and bids price up; a surplus makes sellers compete and pushes price down, until equilibrium returns.

## compare Shifts and the new equilibrium | 49-51
| Change | Example in the text | Equilibrium price | Equilibrium quantity |
| * Demand rises (shift right) | Higher incomes -> more chicken | Up | Up |
| Demand falls (shift left) | Winter -> fewer room coolers | Down | Down |
| * Supply rises (shift right) | New regional factory -> more mobile phones | Down | Up |
| Supply falls (shift left) | Higher input prices -> fewer cars | Up | Down |
- tip Demand shift: price and quantity move the SAME way. Supply shift: they move in OPPOSITE ways.
- ! Both curves move: the bigger shift decides price - demand falling more than supply gives lower price and lower quantity; supply falling more than demand gives higher price and lower quantity.
- ! Butter price rises -> margarine (substitute) demand rises -> margarine price and quantity both rise.
- ! More leather demand abroad -> more beef supplied at home -> beef price falls, quantity rises.

## concept Perishable vs durable goods and reservation price | 51-52
- * Perishable goods (dairy, fruit, vegetables) cannot be stored, so supply is fixed for the period - the supply curve is vertical and price moves only with demand.
- * Durable goods (machinery, motorbikes, mobile handsets, printers) can be stored: supply slopes up while stock can be released, then turns vertical once stock is used up, after which rising demand pushes price sharply.
- * def Reservation price :: The lowest price at which a seller will part with a good or service; offers above it are accepted, below it refused.
- ! Self-test: cost of production, substitutes and consumer income are all treated as factors in the reservation price.

## process Price instability and buffer stocks | 52-54
- * Free markets can be unfair: big landlords hoarding cotton push its price up for textile mills; a hidden sugar-mill cartel refusing to buy cane leaves farmers with a surplus and low prices.
- * Farm price and income swings come from differing elasticities plus unpredictable harvests; food demand is inelastic, so supply shifts cause big price swings - a good harvest can still cut farmers' income.
- def Price ceiling vs price floor :: A ceiling is a legal maximum price, a floor a legal minimum; government usually picks one or the other for a given good.
- * Buffer stock: government guarantees a minimum (floor) price above the market rate and buys up the surplus.
- eg Supply shifts S1 to S2, so the free price would fall to P*; government holds price at P1 and buys the surplus Q1 - Q2, releasing stock later if a shortage pushes price up.
- x Drawbacks: high opportunity cost of the funds, over-production (price signal switched off), administrative and storage costs.
- ! Minimum price always above equilibrium -> surplus; maximum price below equilibrium -> shortage; minimum wage above equilibrium -> surplus labour, lower labour demand, unemployment.

## concept E-business and market price | 55-56
- def E-business :: Buying, selling and exchanging business information through digital systems such as the internet - any time, anywhere.
- * Benefits for firms: customer comparison data, lower inventory and storage cost, global partners, faster and cheaper marketing, little need for bricks-and-mortar.
- * Benefits for customers: doorstep delivery, reverse auctions, wider choice (more consumer sovereignty), easy price comparison, better selection.
- * Price effects: buyers see competitors' floor prices, so firms cannot overcharge; lower overheads allow keener prices; brand loyalty weakens; targeted digital marketing can still justify higher prices; buying anytime, anywhere shifts demand more often.

## exam How this chapter is tested in the CBE | 39-65
- ! MCQ classic: name the event - own-price change = movement (extension / contraction); any other cause = shift (rise / fall). COVID oil example = fall in demand; face-mask boom = rise in demand.
- ! Predict price and quantity after a shift, including two-shift questions where the larger shift wins.
- ! Numeric entry: add individual schedules for market demand; read equilibrium from a table and compute surplus (Qs - Qd) or shortage (Qd - Qs) at a given price.
- ! Policy items: ceiling below equilibrium -> shortage; floor / support price above equilibrium -> surplus; buffer stock aims and drawbacks.
- ! Shape items: perishable supply vertical; land supply vertical; demand for land and factors is derived.

## mistakes Common MCQ traps | 42-54
- x Calling an own-price change a "rise in demand" - it is an extension in quantity demanded.
- x Saying a fall in a substitute's price raises demand for the other good - it lowers it.
- x Treating the whole stock as supply - only the amount offered at that price counts.
- x Placing a support price below equilibrium - it is always set above it.
- x Assuming a demand rise lowers price - only a supply rise does that.

## recall Quick recall | 38-58
- Demand = willingness plus ability to buy.
- Law of demand: price and Qd inverse; income and substitution effects.
- Market demand = horizontal sum of individual demand.
- Own price -> movement; other factors -> shift.
- Substitutes replace; complements go together.
- Direct demand = consumer goods; derived = factors.
- Supply = quantity offered, not stock.
- Law of supply: price and Qs direct.
- Equilibrium: Qd = Qs; surplus pushes price down, shortage up.
- Demand shift: P and Q same direction; supply shift: opposite.
- Perishable supply vertical; price follows demand.
- Reservation price = seller's lowest acceptable price.
- Buffer stock = floor price plus government buying surplus.
- Ceiling = legal max, floor = legal min.
- E-business: more information, keener prices, weaker brand loyalty.
