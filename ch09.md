## concept Barter and its problems | 238-239
- def Barter :: Swapping one economic good or service directly for another, without money.
- * Problems that pushed society towards money:
  - Coincidence of wants - both sides must want what the other offers at the same time (rice holder must find a cotton holder who wants rice).
  - Rate of exchange - hard to agree a value for one good in terms of every other (eggs in apples, rice, phones...).
  - Indivisibility - a chair cannot be split to buy a few eggs.
  - Store of value - perishable goods (e.g. sugarcane) cannot be saved for old age.
  - Transfer of wealth - immovable assets (land in Faisalabad) cannot be moved to Karachi.
  - Tax collection - tax paid in wheat cannot build a road or hospital.
- ! Medium of exchange solves the double coincidence of wants problem.

## concept Money, its functions and schools of thought | 240-241
- def Money (Crowther) :: Anything generally accepted as a means of exchange that also serves as a measure of value and a store of value; broadly, whatever is used to pay for goods, services or factor rewards.
- * Four functions:
  - Medium of exchange - trade without barter.
  - Unit of account - compare relative prices in one common unit.
  - Store of value - give up consumption now and use the surplus later.
  - Standard of deferred payments - consume now, pay in future periods.
- x "Hedge against inflation" and "payment of interest" are NOT functions of money.

| School | Role of money |
| Classical | Passive lubricant between buyers and sellers; markets balance themselves (money neutral) |
| * Keynesian | Also a store of value - can be held to buy goods later, rejecting neutrality |
| Monetarist | Branch of new classicals; spending is influenced by the market rate of interest, so money affects output in the short run, but extra money to lift demand ends in inflation |

## process Evolution and qualities of money | 241-242
- * Barter -> commodity -> metallic -> paper -> credit -> electronic.
- Commodity money: animals, stones, bones, tobacco, arrows - still poor as store / measure of value and immobile.
- * Metallic money: gold and silver, later minted into standard coins.
  - def Full bodied money :: Coin whose metal (intrinsic) value equals its face value.
  - def Token money :: Coin whose intrinsic value is below its face value.
- * Paper money: began with goldsmiths' receipts; central bank later took over issue.
  - Convertible - government promises gold on demand; inconvertible - no gold promise.
- def Fiat money :: Government-issued legal tender with no intrinsic value and no commodity backing - accepted because the government orders it.
- def Credit money :: Any monetary claim on a natural or legal person usable to buy goods and services, e.g. verbal or written agreements, bonds - built on trust that it will be repaid.
- Electronic / digital money: online transactions, debit and credit cards, value stored on devices; fast long-distance payment.
- eg Self-test: credit money risks bad debts, lack of trust and inflation.

- Characteristics of good money:
  - * General acceptability - accepted now and when you pay it on.
  - * Stability - unstable value cannot measure value or serve deferred payments.
  - * Durability - keeps shape and substance; issuing government must also endure.
  - * Divisibility - splits into small units.
  - * Transportability - paper moves easily; concrete blocks would not.
  - * Non-counterfeitability - hard to copy; governments use watermarks etc.
- ! Stable value is NOT an advantage claimed for paper money in the self-test.

## concept Demand for money and its determinants | 243-245
- def Demand for money :: Other things equal, the amount people want to hold as cash or bank deposits (liquid form) rather than invest, at a given time and at various interest rates.
- * Interest rate - the opportunity cost of holding money; higher rate -> lower demand (movement along the curve).
  - eg Mr Zain's Rs 1,000,000: rate rising from 10% to 20% makes lending more attractive than holding cash.
- * Price level - 10% inflation means a Rs 100 meal now needs Rs 110 of money.
- * Real GDP - higher income, more spending, more money demanded (curve shifts right).
- * Financial innovation - ATMs, online transfers, cards reduce cash needed (curve shifts left).
- x Marginal efficiency of capital and special deposits are not determinants in this chapter.

## formula Money demand schedule and shifts | 244-245
| Interest rate (%) | Money demanded (Rs bn) |
| 10 | 100 |
| 8 | 200 |
| 6 | 300 |
| 4 | 400 |
| 2 | 500 |
- f Slope of the schedule :: Change in money demanded / change in interest rate :: Money demand in Rs bn; rate in percentage points :: 10% -> 2% is -8 points; demand 100 -> 500 is +400 -> 400 / -8 = -50 bn per point :: Negative sign = inverse relationship
- * Change in interest rate = movement along MD; change in income, prices or financial innovation = shift (MD0 to MD1 right, MD0 to MD2 left).
- ! "Which factor will NOT shift the money demand curve?" -> the rate of interest.

## concept Keynesian liquidity preference | 246
- * People prefer liquidity (cash) and must be paid a premium to hold illiquid assets - e.g. a Rs 1,000 loan repayable in a year carries more risk than one repaid tomorrow.
- * Total MD = transactions + precautionary + speculative demand.
| Motive | Purpose | Link to interest rate |
| Transactions | Regular day-to-day payments; varies directly with nominal income | Not affected (perfectly inelastic) |
| Precautionary | Emergencies - accident, illness | Inverse but relatively inelastic |
| * Speculative | Hold money to profit from investing (bonds, stocks) later | Inverse and relatively elastic - most sensitive |
- * Keynes: wealth held as cash or bonds; bond prices move inversely with interest rates.
- def Bond :: An investment bought up front that pays a fixed amount back at regular intervals, usually annually.

## concept Liquidity trap | 246-247
- def Liquidity trap :: A state where interest rates are already very low (the critical rate) and liquidity preference is high, so adding money supply cannot push rates lower and monetary policy stops working.
- * People hoard cash, waiting for a better time to buy bonds; MS shifts right along the flat part of MD without lowering the rate.
- * Ways out:
  - Fiscal policy - e.g. a larger budget deficit to raise demand.
  - Higher inflation expectations - hoarded cash loses real value, so spending rises.
  - Expected rise in interest rates - government borrowing from banks lifts rates, people buy bonds, funds flow back into the financial system.
- eg Self-test: causes include depression, deflation and lack of business confidence (not inflation).

## formula Money supply measures | 248
- def Money supply :: Total stock of money circulating in a country at a point in time - notes, coins, bank demand deposits; unlike goods, it is not used up by spending.
- f Money supply aggregates :: M1 = M0 + demand deposits; M2 = M1 + time deposits; M3 = M2 + long-term securities :: M0 = notes and coins; demand deposits = instant access; time deposits = savings not instantly accessible; M3 adds mutual funds and securities :: M0 50, demand deposits 30, time deposits 40, long-term securities 20 -> M1 80, M2 120, M3 140 :: Each wider measure ADDS to the previous one
- * Ranked by liquidity: M0 fully liquid; M3 assets least liquid.
- * Money supply must be limited to keep value, so control rests with the government; more money lowers borrowing rates, raising investment and consumption.

## process Interest rate determination and changes in money supply | 248-250
- * Keynes: MS fixed by the central bank (vertical line); MD slopes down; intersection E sets the interest rate r.
- * Contractionary (high inflation): 1 central bank sells securities -> 2 money flows to central bank, bank reserves fall -> 3 less lending power -> 4 money supply falls (MS to MS2) -> 5 interest rate rises (r2).
- * Expansionary (deflation): central bank buys securities -> reserves rise -> money supply rises (MS to MS1) -> interest rate falls (r1).
- ! More money supply -> lower interest rate -> higher bond and financial asset prices.
- ! Tools to control money supply: open market operations, bank rate, government borrowing.

## exam How PRC tests this chapter | 238-260
- ! Matching MCQs: barter problem to function (coincidence of wants -> medium of exchange); definition to money type (token, full bodied, fiat, credit, convertible).
- ! Elasticity of motives: transactions = interest-inelastic, set by income; speculative = most interest-sensitive; the Keynesian motives are transactions, precautionary, speculative - "investment motive" is a trap.
- ! Aggregates: mutual funds -> M3; build M1, M2, M3 by adding layers in numeric entry.
- ! Direction questions: central bank selling securities or raising bank rate shrinks money supply; buying securities expands it; interest rate is set by money demand and supply.
- ! Self-test also uses velocity of circulation = GDP / money supply.
- ! "NOT" lists: hedge against inflation / payment of interest (not functions); interest rate (does not shift MD); inflation (not a liquidity trap cause).

## mistakes Common mistakes | 238-250
- x Saying the transactions motive depends on the interest rate.
- x Treating an interest-rate change as a shift of the money demand curve.
- x Thinking open-market sales increase money supply.
- x Mixing token money (intrinsic < face) with full bodied money (intrinsic = face).
- x Placing time deposits in M1 or mutual funds in M2.
- x Believing more money supply always lowers interest rates - not in a liquidity trap.

## recall Quick recall | 238-252
- Barter fails: coincidence of wants, exchange rate, indivisibility, storage, transfer, tax.
- Functions: medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value, deferred payments.
- Good money: acceptable, stable, durable, divisible, portable, hard to forge.
- Commodity -> metallic -> paper -> credit -> electronic.
- Token: intrinsic < face; full bodied: intrinsic = face.
- Fiat: no intrinsic value, legal tender.
- MD inversely related to interest rate.
- MD shifters: income, prices, financial innovation.
- Keynes motives: transactions, precautionary, speculative.
- Speculative most elastic; transactions inelastic.
- Liquidity trap: low rates, monetary policy ineffective.
- M1 = M0 + demand; M2 = M1 + time; M3 = M2 + long-term securities.
- Sell securities -> MS falls -> rate rises.
- Interest rate set where MS meets MD.
