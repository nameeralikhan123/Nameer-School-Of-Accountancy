## def What inflation means | 262
- * def Inflation :: A sustained increase in the general price level of a chosen basket of goods and services, which means each rupee buys less in real terms.
- * Four features to keep in mind:
  - It is an absolute rise in the price level, not a relative one.
  - It covers prices of goods and services only, not prices of financial instruments.
  - It looks at the average price of a basket, not one particular good.
  - The rise must be persistent, not a one-year blip.
- Coulborn: too much money chasing too few goods. Samuelson-Nordhaus: a rise in the general level of prices.
- * Value of money and inflation move inversely - higher prices mean lower purchasing power.
- Inflation rate is measured on an annual basis.

## formula Measuring inflation - the CPI and its limits | 262-263
- * def Consumer Price Index (CPI) :: A weighted average of the prices of a fixed basket of goods and services, expressed relative to a base of 100.
- Each item's price change is averaged and weighted by its importance to the cost of living (food is bought daily, so it carries more weight than a house bought rarely).
- * f Price index of one item :: New price / base price x 100 :: Base price = price in the base period :: Bread Rs 150 -> Rs 165: 165 / 150 x 100 = 110 (a 10% rise) :: The index is 110, not 10
- * f Weighted price index :: Sum of (item index x weight) / sum of weights :: Weights = relative importance of each item in the basket :: Food 104x22 = 2,288; clothing 105x18 = 1,890; housing 98x15 = 1,470; transport 15x14 = 210; health 95x5 = 475; education 108x20 = 2,160; leisure 109x6 = 654. Total 9,147 / 100 = 91.47, rounded 91.5 :: Divide by the sum of WEIGHTS (100), not by the number of items (7)
- * f Rate of inflation :: (Index this year - index last year) / index last year x 100 :: Index last year = the comparison (base) period :: (91.5 - 89.5) / 89.5 x 100 = 2.23%, about 2.2% :: Divide by LAST year's index, not this year's (2 / 91.5 = 2.19% is the wrong route)
- * Limitations of the CPI
  - Not fully representative - an average basket does not match what singles, couples, rich or poor households actually buy.
  - Changing quality - a price rise caused by a better product cannot be separated from a pure cost-driven rise.
  - Index-number problem - the choice of base year is debatable and can change the figures a lot.
  - Spending pattern - individual spending patterns are ignored.
- ! "Future expectations" is not listed as a CPI distortion in the text - a classic odd-one-out option.

## compare Degrees of inflation | 263
| Degree | Meaning in the text |
| * Moderate (low) inflation | Roughly 1% to 20% a year |
| Creeping inflation | Low end of moderate - below 5% a year |
| Trotting inflation | Moderate inflation above 5% a year |
| * Hyperinflation | Uncontrollable, extremely rapid rise even within weeks or a month; money stops being a store of value and people hoard goods for barter |
| Deflation | The opposite - average price level falls persistently |
| * Stagflation | Slow growth plus high unemployment together with higher inflation and falling GDP |
- eg Zimbabwe's 2008 hyperinflation was estimated at about 79,600,000,000%.
- tip Creep (<5) then trot (>5) - a walker speeding up before hyperinflation's sprint.

## concept Costs and effects of inflation | 264
- * Debtors gain (they repay money worth less in real terms); creditors lose (they receive less in real terms).
  - f Real interest rate :: Nominal interest - inflation :: Nominal = rate quoted on the loan :: Loan at 12% with 9% inflation -> real return 12 - 9 = 3% :: Lenders suffer when inflation exceeds the nominal rate (real rate negative)
- Entrepreneurs - high, volatile inflation creates uncertainty so big projects are postponed.
- * Fixed-income groups - regressive effect on low-income families and the elderly, especially when food and utility prices jump.
- Investors - governments may respond with higher interest rates, raising borrowing costs and choking investment.
- Government - must spend more to protect purchasing power, may have to cut expenditure, and development slows.
- Competitiveness - the country with lower inflation sells comparable goods more cheaply abroad.

## compare Demand pull vs cost push inflation and the wage-price spiral | 264-265
| Point | Demand pull | Cost push |
| * Origin | Rising aggregate demand (AD) pulls prices up | Rising costs of production (supply side) |
| Diagram | AD shifts right | SRAS shifts up / left against a vertical LRAS, price P1 to P2 |
| * Causes | Fiscal stimulus (multiplier magnifies it); monetary stimulus (lower interest rates); exchange-rate depreciation making exports cheaper; fast growth abroad raising export demand | Rising wages (often when unemployment is low); inflation expectations; dearer raw materials / components; higher indirect taxes; costlier imports after currency falls |
| * Remedies | Raise interest rates; raise taxes (VAT, direct taxes) to cut disposable income; reduce money supply | Limit wage rises; limit utility / energy costs; let the currency appreciate to cheapen imports; subsidies and lower indirect taxes |
- Demand pull becomes sharper when supply does not respond, and most severe once full employment is reached.
- ! Currency devaluation appears on BOTH sides: it raises AD (cheaper exports) and raises import costs (imported cost push).
- ! Higher INDIRECT taxes cause cost push; higher DIRECT taxes are a demand-pull remedy.
- x Government spending on labour development is not a cost-push cause.

- * Higher wages -> more disposable income -> more demand -> higher prices -> workers demand higher wages again.
- * Expectations feed it: workers expecting inflation push for higher pay to protect real income, raising costs and making the expected inflation happen.
- It is called a spiral because it keeps perpetuating itself.

## formula Unemployment - meaning and measurement | 266
- * def Unemployment :: The position of a person who is able to work and is actively looking for a job but cannot find one.
- High unemployment signals economic crisis; high employment signals prosperity. Effects are economic, social and political.
- * f Unemployment rate :: Unemployed labour force / total labour force x 100 :: Total labour force = employed + unemployed actively seeking work :: 6 million unemployed out of a labour force of 75 million -> 6 / 75 x 100 = 8% :: The denominator is the labour force, not the whole population

## compare Types of unemployment | 266
| Type | Run | What causes it |
| * Demand-deficient (cyclical) | Long run | Recession or slow growth - AD too low, firms cut output and labour |
| * Structural | Long run | Labour-market inefficiency - skills mismatched to jobs in certain locations; worse if workers will not relocate or firms will not retrain |
| * Real wage (classical) | Long run | Wages held artificially high by strong unions or a minimum wage above market |
| Seasonal | Short run | Seasonal variation in work |
| * Frictional | Short run | People between jobs or searching for one |
| Voluntary | Short run | People choose not to work at the going wage (e.g. benefits beat taxed pay) |
- eg Typists who used old typewriters for decades become misfits when the firm switches to computers - structural unemployment.
- eg Minimum wage Wm above market W1: labour supplied exceeds labour demanded, unemployment = OSL - ODL.
- tip Structural = wrong skills in the wrong place; frictional = between jobs; real wage = pay floor too high.

## concept Effects of and remedies for unemployment | 267
- * Effects: lost GDP and tax revenue; more poverty; wider income inequality; lower output worsens the current account deficit; rise in crime ("empty minds are devil's workshops").
- * Remedies:
  - Labour-intensive technology to lift labour demand.
  - Agricultural reform and multiple cropping against seasonal unemployment.
  - On-job and off-job training to keep up with technological change (structural).
  - Self-employment schemes backed by financial and technical support.
  - Population control, since rapid population growth swells unemployment.

## concept Phillips curve and natural rate | 268
- * William Phillips (New Zealand, 1958) studied the historical link between unemployment and the rate of change of money wages.
- * Short-run Phillips curve: an inverse relationship - the trade-off between inflation and unemployment.
- Logic: government spending to create jobs lifts labour demand, wages (especially skilled) rise, and firms pass this on as cost push inflation.
- * Lower unemployment comes with higher inflation; lower inflation comes with higher unemployment.
- * def Natural rate of unemployment :: The unavoidable floor of unemployment made up of frictional (treated as voluntary) and structural (technology mismatch) unemployment.
- These people are treated as voluntarily unemployed - in theory they could move to another region and find work.
- x The natural rate is frictional + structural, NOT frictional + cyclical or cyclical + structural.

## exam How this is tested in the CBE | 262-278
- ! Numeric entry: single-item index (new / base x 100), weighted CPI (sum of index x weight / sum of weights) and inflation rate (change / last year's index x 100). Round only at the end of each stage as the question asks.
- ! Classify a cause: interest-rate cut, fiscal stimulus, faster growth abroad = demand pull; wages, indirect taxes, oil / raw materials, dearer imports = cost push.
- ! Match a remedy to the type: demand pull -> higher interest rates, direct taxes, lower money supply ("all of the above" is often right); cost push -> wage limits, energy price limits, subsidies, appreciation.
- ! Name the situation: rising unemployment with inflation = stagflation; below 5% = creeping; 1-20% = moderate; self-feeding wages and prices = wage-price spiral.
- ! Unemployment types: recession = demand-deficient / cyclical (both labels can be one answer); high wage floor = real wage; skills mismatch = structural.
- ! Fill-in-the-blank on structural unemployment: inefficiency of the labour market, misalignment of skill sets, geographical locations.
- ! Who benefits from inflation? Debtors - not creditors or fixed-income groups.

## mistakes Common traps | 262-268
- x Dividing the weighted total by the number of items instead of the sum of weights.
- x Using this year's index as the denominator for the inflation rate.
- x Treating a rise in direct taxes as a cause of inflation - it reduces AD.
- x Saying creditors gain from inflation.
- x Calling unemployment caused by a high minimum wage "structural" - it is real wage (classical).
- x Thinking the Phillips trade-off says both inflation and unemployment fall together.

## recall Quick recall | 262-270
- Inflation = persistent rise in general price level; value of money falls.
- CPI = weighted average price of a basket, base 100.
- Weighted index = sum(index x weight) / sum(weights).
- Inflation rate = change in index / last year's index x 100.
- CPI limits: representativeness, quality, base year, spending pattern.
- Creeping < 5%; trotting > 5%; moderate 1-20%.
- Stagflation = stagnation + unemployment + inflation.
- Real interest = nominal - inflation; debtors gain.
- Demand pull = AD up; cost push = SRAS left.
- Wage-price spiral is self-perpetuating.
- Unemployment rate = unemployed / labour force x 100.
- Long run: demand-deficient, structural, real wage.
- Short run: seasonal, frictional, voluntary.
- Phillips curve (1958): short-run inverse trade-off.
- Natural rate = frictional + structural.
