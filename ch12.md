## def Core terms | 299-300
- * def Balance of payments (BOP) :: A complete yearly record of all economic transactions between one country and the rest of the world - goods, services and capital flows.
- * def Balance of trade (BOT) :: The yearly balance of a country's exports and imports of visible (tangible) goods only.
- Exports (X) earn foreign exchange; imports (M) spend it. Capital also moves as loans, grants and direct investment.
- * BOP surplus = receipts > payments; BOP deficit = receipts < payments (an unfavourable balance).
- BOP information guides changes in monetary, fiscal and exchange-rate policy.

## compare Visibles vs invisibles - BOT vs BOP | 300
| Point | Visible goods | Invisible items |
| * Nature | Tangible - cars, machinery, shoes | Mostly intangible - financial services, insurance, capital flows |
| Measurement | Recorded through customs duties; value measurable | Harder to grasp but still money flowing in and out |
| * Covered by | Both BOT and BOP | BOP only |
- * BOP is the wider term: it contains visibles AND invisibles; BOT is the visible-goods part only.
- eg Education and tourism services are trade in invisibles.

## concept Structure of the BOP | 300-301
- * Three parts: current account, capital account, official financing (financial) account.
- * Current account - current trade with the rest of the world:
  - Trade in goods - finished, semi-finished goods and assembly components.
  - Trade in services - tourism, financial services, consultancy.
  - Investment income - interest, earnings of subsidiaries, dividends on foreign shares flowing home.
  - Transfers - e.g. overseas aid.
- * Capital and financial account - flows of capital and finance:
  - Real foreign direct investment - a domestic firm building and running a factory abroad.
  - Portfolio investment - buying shares in an existing business with no control over it.
  - Financial derivatives - instruments valued from another asset.
  - Reserve assets - foreign financial assets the central bank uses to cover deficits.
- ! Financial derivatives belong to the financial account, not the current account; trade in services is not part of the financing account.
- ! Inflow of capital investment by multinationals is NOT a current account item.

## formula BOP worked illustration | 302
- * f Current account balance :: Visible trade balance + invisible (services) trade balance + net income flows :: Net income flows = net investment income + net transfers :: Goods 10 - 22 = -12; services 11 - 29 = -18; investment income 16 - 12 = +4, transfers -24, so net income -20; current account -12 - 18 - 20 = Rs -50bn :: Net transfers sit inside net income flows - do not count them twice
- * f Financial account balance :: Net direct investment + net portfolio investment + reserve asset transfer + net errors and omissions + reserve funding :: All amounts are net flows :: 30 - 25 + 10 + 2 + 18 = Rs +35bn :: Portfolio investment here is negative (-25)
- f Overall BOP :: Current account + capital account + financial account :: Capital account = net capital transfers :: -50 + 15 + 35 = 0 (capital and financial accounts together +50 offset the -50 current deficit) :: The overall total must come to zero
- eg Pakistan (SBP data, US$ m): 2017-18 current account (A + B + C) = -37,329 - 5,437 + 23,571 = -19,195; 2024-25 = -21,046 - 6,524 + 29,429 = +1,859, a surplus. Secondary income is consistently the large positive item.

## rule Errors, omissions and balancing | 304
- * def Net errors and omissions :: The balancing figure added so that the BOP accounts sum to zero when, in practice, the recorded accounts do not match exactly.
- * A current account deficit must be matched by a surplus on the capital / financial side.
| Position | How it is balanced |
| * Deficit | Sell gold or other financial reserves; borrow from other central banks |
| * Surplus | Buy gold or other financial reserves; pay off debts |
- x Buying gold and paying off debts balance a SURPLUS, not a deficit.

## concept Importance of the BOP | 304
- * Shows the direction of economic performance and guides major policy decisions.
- A deficit points to weak areas - e.g. work on export quantity or quality.
- Guides protection policy - a deficit may justify import restrictions or subsidies.
- Guides budget allocation towards poorly performing sectors.
- Shapes fiscal and monetary policy - e.g. lower interest rates to boost investment, or more spending to support production.
- * Underpins devaluation policy - devaluing the home currency can encourage exports and discourage imports.

## formula Terms of trade (TOT) | 305
- * def Terms of trade :: The ratio of export prices to import prices, showing how many imports one unit of exports can buy.
- * f Terms of trade :: Export price index (current year) / import price index (current year) x 100 :: Price indices measured against a common base of 100 :: Export prices +20% and import prices +10%: 120 / 110 x 100 = 109.09, about 109 - TOT improved by about 9% :: Put EXPORT prices on top; 110 / 120 x 100 = 91.7 is the inverted (wrong) ratio
- * Above 100 = improvement; below 100 = deterioration (a TOT of 112 means a 12% improvement).
- * TOT improves when export prices rise faster than import prices, worsens when import prices rise faster.
- x A better TOT does NOT guarantee a better BOP: TOT is about prices only, the BOP about prices and quantities. Dearer exports can cut export demand by proportionately more, worsening the BOP (and the reverse for a worsening TOT).

## concept Current account deficit - meaning and causes | 306
- * A deficit means a net outflow of demand compared with income coming in - the country is "not paying its way".
- It need not balance on its own because the capital account can run a surplus, though that can rely on unsustainable means such as selling reserve assets.
- * Causes:
  - High income elasticity of demand for imports - strong spending pulls in imports fast.
  - Long-term decline in manufacturing capacity - less to export.
  - Swings in commodity prices when a country imports much of its raw material.
  - * Inelastic imports - a major reason.
  - Domestic inflation - residents switch to cheaper imports.
- eg Importing finished goods and exporting primary goods also points towards a deficit.

## compare Correcting a current account deficit | 306-307
| Measure | Type | How it helps |
| * Tariffs | Non-monetary | Duty raises import prices (WP to WP + T); domestic supply rises and imports shrink from Qs1 - Qd1 to Qs2 - Qd2 |
| Quotas | Non-monetary | A fixed ceiling on quantity imported |
| Export promotion | Non-monetary | Exhibitions, trade fairs and diplomatic deals |
| Import substitution | Non-monetary | Produce at home via training, subsidies, tax support |
| Deflation | Monetary | Lower domestic prices make exports more attractive |
| Exchange control | Monetary | Exporters surrender foreign exchange to the central bank, limiting imports |
| * Exchange-rate depreciation | Monetary | More rupees per dollar: exports cheaper abroad, imports dearer at home |
- * f Foreign price of an export :: Domestic price / exchange rate (Rs per US$) :: Exchange rate = rupees needed for one dollar :: Car Rs 40,000 at 1:4 = $10,000; after depreciation to 1:5 = $8,000 :: Depreciation LOWERS the dollar price of exports - it does not raise it
- ! A tariff immediately raises the cost of imports; a quota limits quantity rather than price.

## exam How this is tested in the CBE | 300-313
- ! Numeric entry: TOT = export index / import index x 100 (round to 2 dp unless told); dollar price of an export after a change in exchange rate; filling a missing line in a BOP table using the rule that the accounts net to zero.
- ! Classification MCQs: which items sit in the current account (goods, services, investment income, transfers) vs the capital / financial account (FDI, portfolio, derivatives, reserve assets).
- ! Debit items on the current account include interest paid on debts owed abroad; tourist spending in the country is a credit.

- ! Interpret TOT: above 100 improved, below 100 deteriorated; improvement in TOT is NOT necessarily an improvement in the BOP.
- ! Fill-in: terms of trade = ratio of export price index to import price index; the amount of imported goods bought per unit of exports; improves when export prices rise faster than import prices.

## mistakes Common traps | 300-307
- x Using BOT and BOP interchangeably - BOT is visibles only.
- x Putting FDI or portfolio investment in the current account.
- x Inverting the TOT ratio (import / export).
- x Assuming a TOT improvement always improves the BOP.
- x Thinking the current account must balance on its own.
- x Saying depreciation makes exports dearer abroad.

## recall Quick recall | 299-308
- BOP = full yearly record of transactions with the rest of the world.
- BOT = visible goods only; BOP = visibles + invisibles.
- BOP parts: current, capital, financial accounts.
- Current account: goods, services, investment income, transfers.
- Capital / financial: FDI, portfolio, derivatives, reserve assets.
- Overall BOP always nets to zero; errors and omissions balance it.
- Deficit financed by selling reserves or borrowing from central banks.
- Surplus used to buy reserves or repay debts.
- TOT = export price index / import price index x 100.
- TOT > 100 improved; < 100 worsened.
- TOT is prices only; BOP is prices and quantities.
- CAD causes: income-elastic imports, falling manufacturing, commodity swings, inelastic imports, inflation.
- Non-monetary fixes: tariffs, quotas, export promotion, import substitution.
- Monetary fixes: deflation, exchange control, depreciation.
